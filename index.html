A signed contract and a working customer are not the same thing.

The gap between a signature and real usage is where most enterprise relationships quietly fail. I own the post-sale welcome: the first conversation, the discovery that finds out what this customer is actually trying to do, and the onboarding path that takes them from initial setup to the point where the product is earning its keep. Structured, not improvised, because a customer who is guessing at their own rollout is a customer who churns at renewal.

I carried a portfolio of enterprise and mid-market accounts and watched them for the signals that come before a renewal conversation goes badly. Answering the same question twice is a system problem.

Every support organisation has a set of questions it answers over and over, and every one of those is time not spent on the accounts that need judgment. I built the self-service knowledge base so the next customer did not need me for what the last one had asked. On my own initiative I built an AI-powered chatbot and an internal automation tool, both aimed at the same thing: putting what I know somewhere it can be used without me in the room. I also mentored a group of new hires through their first two months, which is the same problem solved with people instead of software.

Case 04

The relationship layer

Tools

When something breaks, the second thing at risk is confidence.

I work with everyone from system administrators to senior leadership, and the same escalation needs to sound different to each of them. What holds it together is being predictable rather than impressive: acknowledge fast so nobody fills the silence with worst cases, make one clear call rather than hedging across three, and debrief the same day in outcomes rather than vocabulary nobody outside the team uses. Trust is built in how you behave on the bad days, not the good ones.
