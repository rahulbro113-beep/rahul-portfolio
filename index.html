What I actually think the job is

A product manager owns what the product does. I own whether it does it for the person who bought it.

That is genuinely how I read this role. Onboarding is not paperwork and customer success is not a satisfaction score. It is product management pointed at one relationship: what the customer thought they were buying, what they actually need, and the distance between the two. Somebody has to own that distance. Usually nobody is assigned to it.

Three years doing it across support, onboarding and adoption. The part I like is the part nobody schedules. The escalation that lands at four in the afternoon. The stakeholder who needs a technical answer in plain English. The question that keeps coming back until someone finally builds something so it stops coming back.

My first support role, and the portfolio was every industry at once.

This was where I learned the job. The client portfolio ran across industries, which meant no two accounts had the same setup, the same vocabulary, or the same idea of what "working" looked like. You cannot lean on pattern-matching in that situation. You have to actually ask what a client is trying to do, because the thing they requested and the thing they need are often two different things.

It also meant talking to a system administrator and a senior leader in the same week about the same problem, and understanding that those are two different conversations. The habit I took out of it was getting fluent in unfamiliar enterprise software fast, because there was never a week where that wasn't required.

By the time I left I could take a problem I had never seen before, reproduce it from the customer's side, and work out what it would actually cost them if it sat unresolved for another week. That last part is what changed how I worked. It turned a ticket from a queue item into a decision about urgency, and it is the habit I have carried into every role since.

Answering the same question twice is a system problem.

Every support organisation has a set of questions it answers over and over, and every one of those is time not spent on the accounts that need judgment. I built the self-service knowledge base so the next customer did not need me for what the last one had asked. On my own initiative I built an AI-powered chatbot and an internal automation tool, both aimed at the same thing: putting what I know somewhere it can be used without me in the room. I also mentored a group of new hires through their first two months, which is the same problem solved with people instead of software.

What changed was the shape of the queue. The tool read the history of our agent conversations and the knowledge base, learned how the team actually phrased things, and worked out which questions kept coming back. It answered those directly, routed the rest to the right team, and raised a ticket automatically when something needed a person. Co-built with our product and engineering teams, it handled over 800 queries and brought ticket volume down by 14%. Specialists stopped spending their day on questions that had already been answered somewhere and moved onto the issues that genuinely needed troubleshooting.

A signed contract and a working customer are not the same thing.

The gap between a signature and real usage is where most enterprise relationships quietly fail. I own the post-sale welcome: the first conversation, the discovery that finds out what this customer is actually trying to do, and the onboarding path that takes them from initial setup to the point where the product is earning its keep. Structured, not improvised, because a customer who is guessing at their own rollout is a customer who churns at renewal.

That account renewed. The AM team did the work on the commercial side, but the reason the conversation was straightforward is that there was nothing in it to be surprised by: expectations had been set during onboarding and the customer already knew what the product was doing for them. Their ticket volume came down as well, which is the clearer signal. A customer who has stopped asking basic questions is a customer whose rollout actually worked.

When something breaks, the second thing at risk is confidence.

I work with everyone from system administrators to senior leadership, and the same escalation needs to sound different to each of them. What holds it together is being predictable rather than impressive: acknowledge fast so nobody fills the silence with worst cases, make one clear call rather than hedging across three, and debrief the same day in outcomes rather than vocabulary nobody outside the team uses. Trust is built in how you behave on the bad days, not the good ones.

The honest version is that I could not always fix it in the moment. What I could do was refuse to guess. I would reproduce the problem in a test environment, confirm the fix actually held, and if it turned out to be a configuration or product issue, document it properly and hand it to engineering rather than leaving the customer to chase it. People forgive a slower answer. They do not forgive a confident wrong one.
